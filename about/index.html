The Story Behind the Work

Learning to See Systems

My first academic training was in mechanical engineering. It taught me to think in systems: to understand how individual components interact, how constraints shape performance, and how models can make complex processes more intelligible. My undergraduate work in computational modelling and simulation gave me an early appreciation for the power—and the limits—of formal representations.

Moving Closer to Decisions

Engineering led me toward a different question: how are analytical models used when decisions involve markets, organizations, incentives, and uncertainty? An MBA in Finance introduced me to strategy, risk, and managerial decision-making. It also shifted my attention from technical systems alone to the institutions and people who interpret and act on information.

Working Inside Organizations

Over several years, I worked in computational engineering, business analysis, market research, digital commerce, sales analytics, and blockchain development. These roles required moving repeatedly between data and action—between what an analysis suggested and what an organization could realistically adopt. I learned that technically sound solutions can fail when they overlook routines, ownership, incentives, governance, or the realities of implementation.

Returning to Research With Practical Questions

A Master’s degree in Data Science and Management at Luiss allowed me to reconnect technical methods with organizational questions. My thesis explored a decentralized and privacy-preserving framework for collaborative AI model training in healthcare, integrating federated learning and blockchain. That project became a bridge between my earlier experiences and my current research: it asked not only how an AI system can be built, but how responsibility, trust, privacy, and accountability can be designed around it.

An Interdisciplinary Research Identity

Today, my work sits primarily in Information Systems and management, while drawing on data science, system design, cybersecurity, and organizational theory. I am also interested in cognitive science and computational social science, particularly where they help explain human judgment, technology use, organizational behaviour, and patterns in social and behavioural data.

I am currently pursuing a PhD in Management at Luiss University under the supervision of Professor Paolo Spagnoletti.

Across disciplines, I return to the same question: how can we design and govern technologies that work not only technically, but organizationally and socially?
